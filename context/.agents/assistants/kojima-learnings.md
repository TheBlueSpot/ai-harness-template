## 2026-05-06: Fast Entry And Recovery Trust Are Quality Signals, Not Optional Extras

Durable learnings:

* Fast start, contextual in-play guidance, replayable objective reminders, and player-paced prompts are still first-order quality signals, because current Game Accessibility Guidelines and Microsoft XAG guidance treat them as direct barriers to understanding the loop, not as optional polish.
* Mid-run difficulty changes, practice-without-failure, remembered settings, and separate audio controls are recovery-trust surfaces: once a player finds the right fix, the next attempt should preserve that fix instead of making them climb back through menus or mixed cue stacks.
* For catalog triage, low-friction entry plus readable reminder recovery is a stronger cross-game quality signal than one-off presentation flair, because it protects the first five minutes and the post-failure return path at the same time.
* Current workflow research did not beat `verify-smoke` as the next shared queue move. The strongest later shared helper is still a `lane-overlap checker` that decides whether a fresh finding is truly new shared work, already covered by an existing skill, or only worth a local note.
* Replayable trace artifacts plus typed summaries are the right workflow direction for this repo, but the leverage is in routing and deduping evidence, not in adding another broad audit lane first.

## 2026-05-06: Good Browser Games Need Legible Choices And Busy-Frame Cue Survival

Evidence:

* Microsoft Learn XAG 114 and the Game Accessibility Guidelines eye-line guidance still support a blocker-first rule on May 6, 2026: players need enough context to understand UI actions, and temporary must-react information should stay near focal play instead of living in the periphery.
* Microsoft Learn XAG 117 and its March 2026 version-history update strengthen the same active-play risk from another side: moving, auto-updating, or background-heavy visual layers can make text and prompts harder to read, and motion-related settings now explicitly include more camera and visual-motion controls.
* Caroux et al. found visual clutter, especially background complexity, slows search, reduces accuracy, and increases fixation cost even in action-game-like scenes; expertise changed strategy but did not erase the clutter penalty.
* Ryan, Rigby, and Przybylski, plus Peng et al., keep pointing at the same enjoyment mechanism: autonomy-supportive and competence-supportive features predict more enjoyment and future play, and manipulated autonomy or competence support increased enjoyment, recommendation, and future-play intent.

Inference:

* The next shared workflow gap is not another broad onboarding pass. It is a `choice-readback` lane for moments where the catalog offers a pick but does not help the player understand the tradeoff, compare it to the current state, or see the payoff after choosing.
* The cheapest shared instrumentation follow-up is a `busy-frame clutter` smoke script, because the repo already has `busy_frame_capture.ts`; the missing step is scoring how background complexity, world occlusion, and overlay competition erase critical cues in the worst live frames.
* Shared playtest capture should grow one `choiceMoments` surface before a full new audit ships. Capture the offered options, what the player thought each would do, what changed after the pick, and whether the payoff stayed legible in active play.
* Keep these bets narrow and instrumentation-first. They support the durable catalog goals of visible next action, competence-preserving mastery, and active-play readability without inventing a new cross-game theory stack.

Durable learnings:

* Shared busy-frame clutter review should stay a smoke lane, not a new capture schema, because current `stressFrames`, `competitionMoments`, `ephemeralMoments`, and `clutter` fields already cover the needed blocker-first evidence.
* The real leverage was workflow wiring, not theory: once the smoke script existed, the important follow-through was making capture-pack and evidence-coverage output expose it directly so later operators stop inferring clutter state from adjacent lanes.
* Choice work remains next only after this ship because it still has a schema gap, not a research gap: current `mastery.choicePoints` rows cannot yet preserve offered options, expected payoff, actual payoff, or after-pick comparison.

Evidence shape:

* Fresh 2026-05-06 recheck still kept XAG 114, XAG 117, XAG 109, clutter research, and SDT research aligned with the same order: ship clutter verdict first, then richer choice capture, then choice-readback audit.
* Repo code surface made the decision cheaper than the alternatives: `busy_frame_capture.ts` already normalized the clutter evidence, while choice-readback still lacked the raw packet it would need to judge tradeoff truth.

Weak-evidence caution:

* One clutter smoke report is still session-scoped. Treat it as blocker-first proof for sampled pressure beats, not as a universal claim that every busy frame in the game or catalog is now readable.

* In rewind-platformers, the puzzle fantasy only lands after the base jump loop feels trustworthy; a small speed lift plus lighter gravity, coyote time, and jump buffering can fix `rewind feels unintuitive` faster than adding more route signage or puzzle text.
* 2026-05-06 web research sharpened the next shared gap: the missing lane is recovery surfaces, not another generic onboarding or readability pass. Check whether players can reopen help, reach controls/objectives and difficulty or assists from pause or death, and make the right change without losing progress.
* 2026-05-06 todo review narrowed the live `settings-and-assists` gap further: remap, hold-toggle, sensitivity, speed relief, and raw motor-tax burden already belong to shipped adjacent lanes. The distinct lane is recovery trust only: live or post-failure reachability, progress-safe changes, reminder/practice recovery, and retry persistence.
* 2026-05-06 shared capture now emits a guarded `settings-and-assists-audit.json` starter and capture-pack handoff for that recovery-trust lane, so settings evidence should be logged once in the shared observation packet and then reused by the focused helper instead of being hand-entered again.
* Keep `settings-and-assists` first, but define it around live recovery trust: replayable help, progress-safe difficulty changes, remap/toggle/sensitivity/game-speed relief, and readable non-core prompts long enough to use those knobs.
* Retention evidence should be read through autonomy and competence, not only content volume: if the player cannot correct a mismatch after real play, churn risk stays high even when the core loop is otherwise solid.

## 2026-05-06: Good Browser Games Need Recovery Knobs, Not Just Strong Core Feel

Durable learnings:

* A good browser-game loop is not just readable goals, sharp feedback, and fast retry. It also needs visible recovery knobs when the default ask is wrong for this player right now: current-objective review, action remap, hold-toggle alternatives, game-speed relief, mid-run difficulty changes, and extendable non-core timers.
* For this catalog, settings and assist surfaces are now a distinct workflow gap. Existing lanes can prove that a game is harsh, unclear, or motor-taxing, but they do not yet judge whether the game exposes the right fix path before players churn.
* The next shared research bets should stay narrow and evidence-first: one `settings-and-assists` audit for difficulty, practice, and recovery knobs; one `control-surface` audit for remap, toggle, sensitivity, and speed options; and one timed-prompt smoke script for disappearing non-core UI timers.
* These settings surfaces matter for taste, not just accessibility compliance. When a player can adjust the exact blocker without losing progress, autonomy and competence stay intact and the underlying loop gets a fairer read.

Evidence shape:

* Microsoft XAG 107, last updated on 2026-03-04, says input barriers extend beyond remapping to speed, complexity, and duration, and points to remappable actions, game-speed control, and toggle or auto-hold alternatives.
* Microsoft XAG 108, last updated on 2026-03-04, recommends multiple difficulty presets plus the ability to change difficulty at any time without losing progress.
* Microsoft XAG 116, last updated on 2026-03-04, says non-core UI time limits should warn players, support extension or disablement, and avoid disappearing critical prompts without a recovery path.
* Game Accessibility Guidelines reinforce the same three surfaces from the player side: in-game remap beats system-only fallback, difficulty changes should remain available during gameplay, and control reminders should be reviewable during play.
* Ryan, Rigby, and Przybylski's self-determination work supports treating these knobs as enjoyment infrastructure, because autonomy and competence predict enjoyment and future play.

Weak-evidence caution:

* Accessibility guidance is strongest for identifying preventable blockers and recovery design, not for proving one exact difficulty curve or assist mix is universally best.
* The SDT evidence supports why repair knobs matter for enjoyment, but it does not by itself rank which knob should come first in every genre; keep the queue centered on the cheapest high-leverage workflow gaps.

Topic: sticky arcade progression works best when visible progress reinforces competence and near-term attainability instead of burying the core loop under reward noise.

* Visible progress is strongest when it points at a goal the player believes they can reach soon through play, not when it is a detached meta meter.

## 2026-04-28: Low-Latency Response Budget Beats Extra Juice

Topic: for action and arcade feel, predictable control-response usually matters before adding more FX, shake, or complexity.

Durable learnings:

* Treat input-to-visible-response budget as core mechanic, not polish pass. If controls feel late, players misread timing, distance, and fairness.
* Latency hurts two layers at once: objective performance and subjective control. Players do worse, then also trust game less.
* Small latency still matters. HCI evidence shows measurable degradation can begin around 16 ms, and effect is not nicely linear.
* For timing-heavy or continuous-control loops, every extra frame counts more than many teams assume. A 60 Hz frame is about 16.7 ms, so one avoidable frame can already be meaningful.
* If game must render continuously, input should be read as late as practical and not wait behind presentation work. Separate input handling is worth biasing toward for action, rhythm, fighting, and tight dodge loops.
* When evaluating feel, ask first: "Did player miss because decision was wrong, or because game answered too late for correction?" This catches fake difficulty caused by sluggish response.
* Design triage rule: before tuning enemy speed, telegraph length, knockback, or juice, verify control-response path. Bad latency can masquerade as bad balance.
* Perspective alone is weak predictor for latency tolerance. Recent controlled work found latency damage across first-person, third-person, and bird's-eye shooting alike, so judge by precision and correction demands, not camera label.

Evidence shape:

* Microsoft guidance for low-latency games says 60 fps loops can add up to 16.7 ms if blocked on sync, and recommends separate-thread input when low latency is critical.

* Claypool and Finkel found cloud-game player performance dropping about 25% per added 100 ms in third-person/avatar-style tests, and observed cloud games behaving more like latency-sensitive first-person games than genre labels would suggest.
* Halbhuber et al. found worse reaction time, accuracy, ease of control, challenge fit, immersion, and enjoyment under higher latency, independent of first-person, third-person, or bird's-eye perspective.

* Most evidence here comes from HCI tasks, cloud gaming, and controlled shooting/pointing studies, not broad shipped browser-arcade catalogs.
* Do not turn "16 ms matters" into rigid universal threshold. Exact tolerance depends on action type, correction window, display refresh, baseline device lag, and whether loop is discrete-timing or continuous steering.

## 2026-04-28: Fast Retry Turns Failure Into Practice

* Keep the corrective context intact across death. Same camera, same controls, and one-button retry help players act on the mistake while it is still fresh.

* Deliberate-practice research centers improvement on clear goals, immediate feedback, and repeated correction of errors, which maps closely to tight retry loops.
* Feedback-timing research distinguishes learning from immediate versus delayed feedback, with immediate feedback strongly tied to rapid reinforcement-style correction.
* Developer-authored platformer writeups explicitly frame the genre's appeal around fail-learn-retry loops and dedicated retry buttons with no lengthy gaps.

* The evidence is mixed across domains. Some non-arcade learning studies find delayed feedback can improve later retention, especially for verbal or recall-heavy tasks. Do not overgeneralize "immediate is always better."
